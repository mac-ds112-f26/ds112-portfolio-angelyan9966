TidyTuesday: Urban Green Space

🌿 How Green Are the World’s Cities?

Exploring urban green space through three connected visualizations

For this TidyTuesday project, I explored how green space is distributed across cities, how it differs across world regions, and whether cities with more green area also provide more green space per person.

About the Data

🌎 Source

UN-Habitat
Urban Indicators Dataset

📍 Observations

Cities and urban areas
across different years

📊 Size

5,600 observations
10 variables

The dataset includes measurements such as the percentage of urban land covered by green area and the amount of green space available per person.

01 · The Big Picture

Question

How is the percentage of green area distributed across cities around the world in 2025?

💡 What I Found

Among cities with available data, many are concentrated around roughly 3%–10% green area.

The distribution is right-skewed, meaning cities with especially high percentages of green space are much less common.

Why this works

  • Makes the overall distribution easy to see
  • Clearly shows where most cities are concentrated

What I would improve

  • Experiment with bin width
  • Add a reference line or annotation

02 · Does Geography Matter?

Question

Do cities in different world regions have different amounts of green area?

💡 What I Found

Green-area percentages vary across world regions.

Some regions show relatively higher values, while others tend to have lower percentages. There is also substantial variation within many regions, meaning cities in the same broad region can still look very different.

Why this works

  • Horizontal layout makes region labels easier to read
  • Boxplots show both typical values and variation

What I would improve

  • Reorder regions for easier comparison
  • Clean up long region labels

03 · Green Space for People

Question

Do cities with more green area also have more green space per person?

💡 What I Found

Cities with higher percentages of green area generally also tend to provide more green space per person.

However, the relationship is not perfect. The scatterplot still contains substantial variation and several outliers.

Why this works

  • Directly compares two quantitative variables
  • Transparency helps reduce overlapping points

What I would improve

  • Label interesting outliers
  • Explore whether population size explains some variation

🌱 Final Takeaway

These three visualizations tell a connected story.

First, high percentages of urban green space are relatively uncommon.

Second, access to green space differs across world regions.

Finally, cities with more green area generally also offer more green space per person — but there are important exceptions.

This project helped me move from a univariate distribution to regional comparison and finally to a relationship between two quantitative variables, while using visualization to build a connected data story.

# 🌿 How Green Are the World's Cities?

### Exploring urban green space through three connected visualizations

For this TidyTuesday project, I explored how green space is distributed
across cities, how it differs across world regions, and whether cities with
more green area also provide more green space per person.

:::


```{r}
#| echo: false
#| message: false
#| warning: false

library(tidyverse)

urban <- read_csv(
  "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rfordatascience/tidytuesday/main/data/2026/2026-09-22/urban.csv"
)

urban_2025 <- urban |>
  filter(year == 2025)
```

# 01 · The Big Picture

::: {.tt-question}

### Question

**How is the percentage of green area distributed across cities around the world in 2025?**

:::


```{r}
#| echo: false
#| fig-cap: "Distribution of green-area percentages across cities in 2025."
#| fig-alt: "Histogram showing that many cities have relatively low percentages of green area, while fewer cities have very high percentages."

ggplot(
  urban_2025,
  aes(x = averageShareOfGreenAreaInCityUrbanAreaPct)
) +
  geom_histogram(
    fill = "#009E73",
    color = "white"
  ) +
  labs(
    title = "Most Cities Have Relatively Low Shares of Green Area",
    subtitle = "Distribution of urban green-area percentages in 2025",
    x = "Average share of green area (%)",
    y = "Number of cities"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()
```


:::: {.columns}

::: {.column width="50%"}

::: {.tt-insight .tt-green-border}

### 💡 What I Found

Among cities with available data, many are concentrated around roughly
**3%–10% green area**.

The distribution is right-skewed, meaning cities with especially high
percentages of green space are much less common.

:::

:::

::: {.tt-mini}

# 02 · Does Geography Matter?

::: {.tt-question}

### Question

**Do cities in different world regions have different amounts of green area?**

:::


```{r}
#| echo: false
#| fig-cap: "Comparison of green-area percentages across SDG world regions in 2025."
#| fig-alt: "Horizontal boxplots comparing green-area percentages across different world regions."

ggplot(
  urban_2025,
  aes(
    x = averageShareOfGreenAreaInCityUrbanAreaPct,
    y = sdgRegion
  )
) +
  geom_boxplot(
    fill = "#0072B2"
  ) +
  labs(
    title = "Green Space Looks Different Across World Regions",
    subtitle = "Distribution of urban green-area percentages by SDG region",
    x = "Green area (%)",
    y = NULL
  ) +
  theme_minimal()
```


:::: {.columns}

::: {.column width="60%"}

::: {.tt-insight .tt-blue-border}

### 💡 What I Found

Green-area percentages vary across world regions.

Some regions show relatively higher values, while others tend to have
lower percentages. There is also substantial variation **within** many
regions, meaning cities in the same broad region can still look very different.

:::

:::

::: {.column width="50%"}

::: {.tt-mini}

### Why this works

- Horizontal layout makes region labels easier to read
- Boxplots show both typical values and variation

### What I would improve

# 03 · Green Space for People

::: {.tt-question}

### Question

**Do cities with more green area also have more green space per person?**

:::


```{r}
#| echo: false
#| fig-cap: "Relationship between green-area percentage and green area per capita among cities in 2025."
#| fig-alt: "Scatterplot showing a generally positive relationship between green-area percentage and green area per capita, with several outliers."

ggplot(
  urban_2025,
  aes(
    x = averageShareOfGreenAreaInCityUrbanAreaPct,
    y = greenAreaPerCapitaM2
  )
) +
  geom_point(
    color = "#0072B2",
    alpha = 0.5
  ) +
  labs(
    title = "More Green Area Often Means More Green Space per Person",
    subtitle = "But cities still vary considerably",
    x = "Green area (%)",
    y = "Green area per capita (m²)"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()
```

::: {.column width="50%"}

::: {.tt-insight .tt-purple-border}

### 💡 What I Found

Cities with higher percentages of green area generally also tend to provide
more green space per person.

However, the relationship is not perfect. The scatterplot still contains
substantial variation and several outliers.
